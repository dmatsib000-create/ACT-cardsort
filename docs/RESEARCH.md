# Research brief: the evidence behind Lodestar

*This brief was compiled in November 2026 to ground Lodestar's content and flow. Citations were checked against PubMed (PMIDs given) where indexed, and against publisher, ACBS (contextualscience.org) or repository records otherwise. Items marked **[snippet-verified]** were confirmed from search-engine excerpts because the primary PDF could not be opened; items marked **[unverified]** could not be confirmed. See "Verification notes" at the end.*

## 1. How ACT defines values

**Core definition.** The standard technical definition comes from Wilson & DuFrene (2009) and is quoted in Smout et al. (2014). Values are "freely chosen, verbally constructed consequences of ongoing, dynamic, evolving patterns of activity, which establish predominant reinforcers for that activity that are intrinsic in engagement in the valued behavioral pattern itself." Wilson & Murrell (2004) set out values work as the thing that gives behavioral treatment its "course" or direction. Hayes, Strosahl & Wilson (2012) put values among the six core processes. Harris (2019) restates them in plain language as "desired qualities of ongoing action": how you want to behave, what you want to stand for, and how you want to treat yourself, others and the world.

Key properties to carry into the app:

| Property | Meaning | App copy implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Chosen / freely held** | A value is chosen, not justified, proven, or imposed. Reinforcement comes from the action itself, not from others' approval. | Avoid "the right values." Say "values you choose to live by." |
| **Ongoing quality of action** | A value is expressed with verbs and adverbs ("acting *lovingly*"). It is not something you possess. | Value cards should be quality words (Caring, Curious, Honest), not outcomes (Wealth, Fame). |
| **Never "completed"** | A value is like a compass direction ("west"). Goals are destinations you can reach along the way. | Never show a "value achieved ✓". Tick off actions and goals only. |
| **Values ≠ goals** | Goals are finite and can be finished. Values are never finished. Wilson et al. (2010) describe values as ongoing patterns and goals as achievable ends that serve them. | Keep "What matters (value)" separate from "What I'll do (goal/action)." |
| **Values ≠ feelings** | Feelings can't be chosen and come and go. Values can be acted on whatever you feel. "Feeling happy" or "feeling confident" is not a value. | Flag cards or user entries that name emotional states and suggest a reframe. |
| **Values ≠ rules / "shoulds"** | Rigid rules ("I *must* always...") and moral "shoulds" turn values into a source of guilt. Values are held lightly and flexibly. | Detect "should/must/have to" in free text and prompt "Is this something you *want* to stand for, or feel you *have* to?" |

**Pliance.** In relational frame theory, *pliance* is rule-following controlled mainly by socially mediated consequences: approval, avoiding disapproval, looking like a "good person" (Hayes et al., 2012). In values work the risk is that a client picks values to please parents, partners, therapists, or culture. Clinicians typically screen for it with questions like these (common ACT clinical heuristics, paraphrased; I did not verify exact wording against the books):

- "If no one would ever know you chose this, would you still choose it?"
- "If your parents or partner approved of anything you chose, would this still be on your list?"
- "Is this something you *want* to be about, or something you think you *should* want?"
- Noticing whether talking about the value brings vitality and lightness, or heaviness, guilt and obligation.

The VLQ instructions also ask respondents to rate what is important to *them*, not what they think they should value or what others think (Wilson et al., 2010).

**Hexaflex position.** The psychological flexibility model ("hexaflex") has six interrelated processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment contact, self-as-context, **values**, and **committed action** (Hayes et al., 2006; Hayes et al., 2012). Values and committed action make up the "engaged" or "activation" side. Values give direction, and committed action is the effective, values-guided behavior that gets built up into larger patterns. Acceptance and defusion clear the way for those actions when uncomfortable thoughts and feelings show up. So a card sort on its own is only half the work. The app should end in action, plus a way to handle barriers.

## 2. Values card sorts

**Personal Values Card Sort (PVCS).** Developed by W. R. Miller, J. C'de Baca, D. B. Matthews & P. L. Wilbourne at the University of New Mexico (2001). It was hosted by UNM's Center on Alcoholism, Substance Abuse, and Addictions (CASAA) and grew out of motivational interviewing practice (it appears as a resource with Miller & Rollnick's MI materials from Guilford).

- **Licensing [snippet-verified]:** The UNM/CASAA PDF states that the instrument is **in the public domain** and may be copied, adapted, and used without permission. The 2011 update (Guilford-hosted copy) carries a similar notice that it "may be copied or adapted without further permission." I could not load the PDFs to quote them character for character. Before you ship, open `casaa.unm.edu/assets/inst/personal-values-card-sort.pdf` and copy the exact sentence into your app's credits.
- **Content [snippet-verified]:** The 2001 sheet numbers its cards up to 83 (the last is "World Peace"). It uses three headers: *Not Important*, *Important*, *Very Important*. Each card is a value name plus a short definition, e.g., "ACCEPTANCE: to be accepted as I am". The 2011 update and CASAA instruction sheet describe a smaller deck (about 50 cards plus blanks) with five anchor piles: *Not important, Somewhat important, Important, Very important, Most important*. That version caps the "Most important" pile at 10.
- **Procedure:** (1) Sort every card into 3–5 importance piles. (2) Take the top pile and narrow it to about 5–10. If the pile is too big or too small, borrow from or prune the next pile. (3) Rank-order the finalists (1 = most central). (4) Talk through each top value: what it means, how it shows up now, and where there's a gap. Blank cards let people add their own values.
- **Caveat for ACT use:** The PVCS comes from MI and includes some outcome- or state-type items, such as wealth, fame, power, and comfort. These don't fit ACT's "quality of action" definition. You can adapt freely, but consider rewording or flagging those items.

**Russ Harris values checklist.** Harris's books (*The Happiness Trap*, *ACT Made Simple*, 2nd ed.) and his free "Extra Bits" worksheet packs include a values checklist. Each value is a quality word with a short gloss, e.g., "Acceptance/self-acceptance: to be accepting of myself, others, life." Readers rate each one Very / Quite / Not important. **Count [unverified]:** I couldn't confirm "58". Secondary sources give about 60. Check the current PDF. **Usage terms [snippet-verified]:** The material is © Russ Harris (the Extra Bits PDF says "© Russ Harris 2019"), and some printed handouts are "reprinted by permission of New Harbinger." His site (actmindfully.com.au) says the handouts and worksheets can be shared *with clients*. I found **no open licence** allowing republication in a public app. → Write your own list, or ask Harris or New Harbinger for permission. The PVCS is the legally safer base.

**Other decks.** Many commercial and free decks exist (ACT-themed printable cards; "Values Cards" from various publishers; MI-derived web sorts). Most are proprietary or adapted from the PVCS. A Utah State University Extension "Forty Common Values" checklist (2021 PDF) also exists, but I didn't check its licence. I verified no other deck's licence and suggest you not rely on any of them.

## 3. Related assessment and exercise tools

| Tool | What it asks | Scoring idea | Free to use / adapt? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Valued Living Questionnaire (VLQ)** (Wilson et al., 2010) | 10 domains: family (not marriage/parenting), marriage/couples/intimate relations, parenting, friends/social life, work, education/training, recreation/fun, spirituality, citizenship/community, physical self-care. Each is rated 1–10 for **importance**, then 1–10 for **consistency** of action in the past week. *(Domain list partly verified in search results; the full list is from the published form.)* | Per domain, importance × consistency. Composite = mean of products (1–100); sources differ on sum vs. mean. The most useful clinical signal is a gap between **high importance and low consistency**. | Distributed free via ACBS for clinical and research use. I found no explicit open licence. Using the *structure* (domains + two ratings) is low-risk. Reproducing it verbatim needs attribution, and ideally the author's OK. |
| **Bull's-Eye Values Survey (BEVS)** (Lundgren et al., 2012) | A dartboard with life domains: work/education, leisure, relationships, personal growth/health. The person writes a value for each domain and marks how close their recent living is to the bull's-eye. Also lists obstacles and planned actions. | Distance from center = values–action discrepancy (larger = less consistent). | Freely circulated via ACBS. Same licence caveat as the VLQ. The concept is easy to build as an original visual. |
| **Valuing Questionnaire (VQ)** (Smout et al., 2014) | General valued living over the past week, not tied to domains. *[Structure from secondary source]*: 10 items rated 0–6, in two subscales, **Progress** and **Obstruction**. | Subscale sums. High Progress and low Obstruction = more valued living. | Hosted on ACBS. Licence unverified. Reilly et al. (2019) rate the VQ among the best-supported values measures. |
| **Engaged Living Scale (ELS)** (Trompetter et al., 2013) | 16 items with two subscales: **Valued Living** (10) and **Life Fulfillment** (6). | Subscale and total sums. Psychometrics are good in nonclinical and chronic-pain samples. | Licence unverified. A 9-item short form exists (Trindade et al., 2015). |
| **Life Compass** (Dahl et al., 2009) | A compass or grid of life domains. For each: what you value, its importance, how much you are acting on it, and the barriers in the way. | Qualitative, plus importance and action ratings per domain. | It's from a copyrighted book (New Harbinger). Adapt the idea, don't copy the worksheet. |
| **Choice Point** (Harris; Harris's handout credits Bailey, Ciarrochi & Harris, 2013) | A fork diagram. At the bottom is a situation plus difficult thoughts and feelings ("hooks"). Up one side are **towards moves** (values-congruent actions), up the other **away moves** (actions that take you away from the life you want). At the base are "unhooking skills" (defusion, acceptance) and values. | No scoring. It's a decision aid. Towards and away are judged from the *user's* perspective, and away moves aren't framed as "bad," just as normal and human. | © Harris. The concept is widely taught, so build your own diagram and wording. |
| **Sweet Spot exercise** (Wilson & Sandoz, 2008) | A slow guided recall of a specific moment that felt deeply meaningful. The person lingers on its sensory detail, then notices what it shows about what they value. | No scoring. Prompts written reflection. | Script ideas are widely shared. Write your own wording. |
| **Tombstone / eulogy / 80th-birthday** | "What would you want written on your tombstone?" or "Imagine your 80th birthday, or your funeral: what do you hope people would say you stood for?" These are long-standing ACT values-clarification exercises, appearing in Hayes et al. (2012) and Harris's books *[exact page/edition not verified]*. | No scoring. Prompts writing. | Generic exercise forms. Write your own text. Use gently, since death imagery can upset people who are grieving or at risk. |

## 4. Evidence

**ACT efficacy overall.**
- **A-Tjak et al. (2015)**, 39 RCTs, N = 1,821. ACT beat control conditions at post-treatment and follow-up (Hedges' g = 0.57). Against waitlist g = 0.82, psychological placebo g = 0.51, treatment as usual g = 0.64. It did not differ significantly from CBT (p = .14). Process-measure effects g = 0.56. *(PubMed PMID 25547522.)*
- **Gloster et al. (2020)** reviewed 20 meta-analyses with 100 controlled effect sizes across 12,477 participants. ACT was efficacious for every condition examined (anxiety, depression, substance use, pain, transdiagnostic). It generally beat inactive controls, TAU and most active treatments, **except CBT**, where results were comparable.

**Values components specifically.**
- **Levin et al. (2012)** meta-analyzed 66 laboratory-based component studies. Values components, and values plus mindfulness components, had significant positive effects versus inactive comparisons. Effects were larger when components used **experiential methods** (metaphors, exercises) rather than a rationale alone. *(PMID 23046777. I couldn't get the values-specific g from the abstract.)*
- **Chase et al. (2013)** randomized students to online goal-setting, online values training plus goal-setting, or waitlist. Only **values plus goal-setting** improved next-semester GPA. Goal-setting alone did not. This directly supports pairing values work with action planning.

**Online, self-guided ACT and apps.**
- **French et al. (2017)**, self-help ACT (book or computer), 13 RCTs, N = 2,580. Small effects: depression g = 0.34, anxiety g = 0.35, psychological flexibility g = 0.42. Guidance improved outcomes, while format did not matter. The authors called the evidence tentative.
- **Thompson et al. (2021)**, internet ACT, 25 studies. Small pooled effects on anxiety, depression, quality of life and flexibility, maintained at follow-up. Therapist guidance improved depression and flexibility outcomes. Mean completion was about 76%, and clinically significant change was limited. *(PMID 33622516.)*
- **Klimczak et al. (2023)**, self-guided online ACT (web and apps), 53 RCTs, N = 10,730. Better than waitlist for anxiety, depression, QoL and flexibility. Against *active* controls, only psychological flexibility and the omnibus outcome were superior. *(PMID 36847182.)*
- **Han & Kim (2022a, 2022b)**, internet ACT. Small effects on depression, anxiety, stress, distress and QoL (39 RCTs). Medium effect on psychological flexibility and small effect on valued living (34 RCTs). *(PMIDs 36485030, 36040783.)*
- **App exemplar: Bricker et al. (2020)**, iCanQuit (ACT smoking-cessation app) vs. NCI QuitGuide, N = 2,415. 12-month 30-day abstinence was 28.2% vs. 21.1% (OR 1.49, 95% CI 1.22–1.83). *(PMID 32955554.)*

**Bottom line for the app:** Self-guided values work is plausibly helpful, with small effects. It works better when it is experiential and leads into concrete goals. Guidance and engagement matter, and none of this replaces therapy.

## 5. Practical guidance for self-guided values work

**Writing a personal value statement.**
1. Start from a chosen card, e.g., "Caring".
2. Pick a domain (relationships, work, health…).
3. Write it as an ongoing quality of action: *"In my relationships, I want to be **caring**: to notice what people need and offer help and warmth."* Use verbs and adverbs, present tense, under your own control.
4. Test it with four questions. Can it ever be "finished"? (If yes, it's a goal.) Is it about how *I* act, not how others act or how I feel? Would I choose it if no one knew? Does it feel vital rather than heavy?

**Value → SMART committed action.** Harris (2019) adapts SMART for ACT: **S**pecific, **M**eaningful (values-guided), **A**daptive (likely to improve life), **R**ealistic (fits your resources), **T**ime-framed. Encourage tiny, immediate actions ("today or this week") as well as larger goals. In the app, each action stores *what, when/where, which value it serves, and the first tiny step*. Pairing values with goal-setting is supported by Chase et al. (2013).

**Barriers and willingness.** Prompt the user to anticipate barriers: (a) **inner** (thoughts like "I'll fail", anxiety, low motivation) and (b) **outer** (time, money, other people). For inner barriers, use ACT's willingness question: *"Am I willing to make room for this discomfort in order to do what matters?"* Briefly coach defusion ("I'm having the thought that…") and acceptance. For outer barriers, problem-solve or shrink the step. A Choice Point screen is a good fit for in-the-moment decisions: name the situation and the hooks, list the towards and away options, then choose.

**Using values for decisions.** For each option, ask which top values it moves you towards or away from, and what you would be willing to feel in order to take it. Present this as a reflection aid, not as an algorithm that hands down an answer.

**Safety for a self-help tool.**
- A clear, persistent disclaimer: educational self-help, **not therapy, diagnosis, or crisis support**. It is not a substitute for a professional.
- Crisis resources reachable from every screen. **US: call or text 988** (988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline; chat at 988lifeline.org; veterans press 1). **Elsewhere: findahelpline.com** (ThroughLine's vetted international directory). Say "If you are in immediate danger, call your local emergency number."
- Content warnings and an opt-out for mortality-themed exercises (tombstone or funeral).
- Never imply a user's values are "wrong" or score them as pathology. Avoid clinical cut-offs. The cut-offs I found for the VLQ come from commercial templates, not validation studies.
- Privacy-first: keep data on the device (localStorage or IndexedDB) and provide export and delete. Tell users not to put identifying or sensitive details in shared exports.

## Design implications for the app

1. **Base the deck on the public-domain PVCS** (cite Miller et al., 2001, with the verbatim notice). Reword or flag outcome or state items (Wealth, Fame, Comfort) into action-quality language, and allow custom cards. Don't copy Harris's checklist text without permission.
2. **Sort flow:** a 3-pile first pass (Very / Somewhat / Not important), then narrow "Very" to 5–10, then drag-rank the top 5. Show a pile cap and let users borrow from or prune piles, as the PVCS does.
3. **Values-quality nudges:** light linting of custom entries for goal-like ("get a promotion"), feeling-like ("be happy"), and rule-like ("should/must") language, with gentle reframes.
4. **Pliance check** for each top value: "Would you choose this if no one ever knew?" and "Is this a *want* or a *should*?" Offer optional reconsideration without judgment.
5. **Reflection step modelled on the VLQ and Bull's-Eye:** for each top value or domain, rate importance (1–10) and recent consistency (1–10). Visualize the gap with an original dartboard or bar, and invite the user to target high-importance, low-consistency areas first. Don't show clinical cut-offs.
6. **Experiential prompts:** an optional Sweet Spot recall, 80th-birthday reflection (with a content warning), and value-statement builder. Levin et al. (2012) found experiential components outperform rationale-only ones.
7. **Committed action builder:** value → domain → SMART action → first tiny step → when/where → anticipated barriers (inner/outer) → willingness statement. Track actions, not values, as "done."
8. **Decision helper:** a Choice-Point-style screen with situation, hooks, towards and away options scored against the user's top values, and a willingness prompt. Use your own diagram and wording.
9. **Engagement:** completion rates for self-guided iACT are moderate, and guidance helps. Make sessions short, resumable, and printable or exportable so a user can bring the results to a therapist.
10. **Safety and privacy:** persistent "not therapy" notice, crisis links (988; findahelpline.com) in the header or footer, all data stored locally with clear export and delete, no analytics trackers.
11. **Citations page:** list the sources below. Mark the VLQ, BEVS, VQ and ELS as "inspired by" unless you get author permission to reproduce items.
