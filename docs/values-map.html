Lodestar Values Map

How Lodestar’s values deck relates to the science of values and wellbeing: what a review of the original 65 cards found, and the 72-card deck now in use. Built from two research briefs covering the philosophy of value, linguistics, and the psychology of values and wellbeing.

The map

The ring is Shalom Schwartz’s circle of 19 basic human values, the most widely replicated map of what people value. It has been found in 49 cultural groups. Values next to each other can be pursued together; values on opposite sides pull against each other. The colored band shows the four families. Each dot is a card, placed in its closest region and colored by what the research suggests doing with it. Dashed rings are cards added in version 2. The center holds cards with no home on the circle: virtues and ACT processes that can serve any value.

What the review found in the original deck

The deck leans hard toward one half of the circle

Almost every card sits on the “growth” side: openness and self-transcendence. That fits ACT, and those values do relate modestly to wellbeing. But people whose priorities are achievement, duty, security or tradition find few cards that describe them honestly. They either over-endorse caring cards or feel judged.

One region is overcrowded

Thirteen cards sit in benevolence: caring. Caring, Kindness, Compassion, Empathy, Love and Nurturing share one core idea, so a person who values care spreads their choices across near-synonyms. That makes the “very important” pile swell and the core values harder to pick.

Whole regions are empty

Power (dominance and resources), face, and societal security have no cards; rules has one. Leaving out power, wealth and image is a sound ACT decision because they are outcomes, not qualities of action. But each has an action form people genuinely hold (having a say, providing for family, doing one’s part) that can be added safely.

Strong wellbeing strengths are missing

Of the five character strengths most strongly linked to life satisfaction (hope, zest, gratitude, love and curiosity), the deck has no card for hope or zest. Purpose, which appears in nearly every wellbeing model, is the third notable absence. It is left out on purpose here, because the whole exercise is a search for purpose and a “Purpose” card would be circular.

Every card is a compliment

All 65 names are praise words, so putting Honesty in “not important to me” feels like saying you don’t care about honesty. This pushes people to rate most cards highly (a ceiling effect), which makes the sort less informative. Adding a few legitimate, less flattering cards and softening the bottom pile’s label both help.

The deck mixes kinds of things

It includes virtues (Honesty), goods (Beauty, Health), a state (Peace), life areas (Family, Friendship, Community) and practices (Learning, Mindfulness). ACT’s own tools treat life areas separately from the qualities you bring to them. The action-phrased descriptions mostly repair this, but people read the name first.

Four lenses on a values deck

Axiology

What kind of value is on each card?

  • Final or instrumental. Two people can rate Health “very important”, one for its own sake and one so they can be there for their children (Korsgaard, 1983). Asking “for its own sake, or for something else?” can reveal the deeper value.
  • Pluralism. Values can be many, can conflict and cannot always be ranked (Berlin; Raz, 1986). Chang (2002) argues two values can be “on a par”. Forcing a strict ranking can feel wrong, so allowing ties respects this.
  • A curated deck is an objective list. ACT says values are freely chosen, yet a fixed deck decides in advance which goods count. Custom cards and a broader deck soften this.
  • Caring as identification. Frankfurt (1971, 1982): what we care about is what we identify with, not what we merely like. A follow-up such as “Do I want to be moved by this, even when I’m not?” fits ACT’s idea of chosen values.

Semantics

What do the words mean, and to whom?

  • Direction. Respect, Trust, Acceptance and Love can be given or received. Being respected is an outcome, not a value. Descriptions should make the person the giver.
  • Polysemy. Love (romantic, family, universal), Order (tidiness or society), Peace (inner, social, political). One-line descriptions pick a sense and can drift from what the word means to a given reader (Rosch, 1975; Fehr, 1988).
  • Culture. Fairness and freedom have no exact equivalents in many languages (Wierzbicka, 1997, 2006). Independence, Assertiveness, Authenticity and Self-care carry an individualist ideal, so relational counterparts like Harmony and Duty widen the deck.
  • Circular words. Descriptions like “what matters” or “what is right” assume the very thing being sorted (Williams, 1985).

Pragmatics

What does choosing a card do?

  • Praise words. Value words carry approval (Stevenson, 1937; Hare, 1952), so rejecting one is socially marked. Some inflation is real valuing, not bias (Schwartz et al., 1997).
  • Double-barreled cards. Cards that bundle two acts or targets are answered for whichever part stands out, and measure less validly (Menold, 2020).
  • Ranking beats rating at the top. Choosing most and least important first reduces end-piling (McCarty & Shrum, 2000). Best-worst choices recover the same structure faster (Lee et al., 2008).
  • Verbs over identities. “Being a helper” can motivate in the moment but backfires after setbacks (Foster-Hanson et al., 2020), and the voter-noun effect failed a large replication (Gerber et al., 2016). Action wording is sturdier.

Wellbeing science

How do values relate to living well?

  • Schwartz’s circle (Schwartz et al., 2012) gives the map’s structure. Openness values relate modestly to higher wellbeing, but effects are small and depend on culture and fit with one’s surroundings (Sortheix & Schwartz, 2017).
  • Intrinsic over extrinsic. Goals for money, image and status relate to lower wellbeing (Kasser & Ryan, 1996; Dittmar et al., 2014, 259 samples), largely through unmet basic needs.
  • Basic needs. Autonomy, competence and relatedness (Ryan & Deci, 2000), plus beneficence, the sense of giving (Martela & Ryan, 2016). The deck covers all four, relatedness most heavily.
  • Do not steer. These findings should shape the deck’s coverage, never nudge people toward “healthy” values. Steering would invite choosing values to please others, which ACT works to avoid.

The deck now in use

These recommendations were adopted as Lodestar’s deck version 2 in November 2026. Applying them gives cards, up from 65. Five merges remove near-duplicates, two splits separate cards that held two values, and eight additions fill the empty and thin regions. Version 3 then split Mastery into Expertise and Resourcefulness, because the word was flagged as one some people hear through “master” and the card held two different ideas. All descriptions follow the wording rules below.

Changes to the sort, now built

A softer bottom pile

The bottom pile is now “Less important to me”. It separates low priority from rejection, so people can use it honestly.

Narrowing a crowded pile

When more than ten cards are very important, an optional round shows a few at a time and asks which matters most and which least, then suggests a top five. It never replaces values someone has already chosen.

Ties when ranking

Two values can be marked “on a par”. They share a rank number and count equally in the decision helper.

What it’s for

Reflect asks, optionally, whether each value is held for its own sake or for what it gives, and what that is. That may be the deeper value.

Laddering up from outcomes

If someone adds a custom card that names an outcome (Wealth, Success) or a feeling (Being happy), a gentle prompt asks what lies underneath. The card can still be added.

Life areas as areas

An optional exercise after the action plan: people pick the life areas that fit them, link up to three values to each, and can say an area is hard right now. Actions can be tagged with an area. No scores are shown.

Every card fits ACT’s definition of a value

In ACT, a value is a freely chosen, desired quality of ongoing action. After version 2 was adopted, every card was checked against that definition. A card must describe how a person acts, never be something that can be finished, stay within the person’s control, not be a feeling, not be a rule, and name one action at a time.

Fourteen cards were reworded in that pass, for example Excellence (“to a high standard” became “Putting care and skill into the work I do”) and Influence (“Having a say”, which depends on others, became “Speaking up and getting involved in decisions”). An automatic check now tests every card against these rules whenever the deck changes.

Life areas

Family, work and health are places where values are lived, not values themselves. A third research brief found strong support in ACT theory for keeping them separate, but warned against scoring them and against long rating grids late in a session. So life areas became a separate, optional layer offered after the action plan. People pick only the areas that fit their life, can add their own, can say an area “matters to me, but it’s hard right now”, and never see a combined or gap score. The Family card stays in the deck, reworded as a quality: “Caring for the people I count as family”.

Wording rules for every card

  1. One act, one target. If a description covers myself and others, or noticing and doing, split it or choose one.
  2. Start with a verb. “Telling the truth” rather than “Being honest”. Identity framing is fragile after lapses, and lapses are certain.
  3. Make the person the giver. Never write a value that depends on how others treat you.
  4. No circular words. Replace “what matters” and “what is right” with concrete scope.
  5. No absolutes by default. “Even when no one is watching” sets a perfectionist bar. Use it only where it defines the value.
  6. No feelings as values. Calm, happy and confident can’t be chosen. Describe the action instead.
  7. Plain, translatable words. Simple vocabulary travels better across cultures and languages.
  8. Show the description everywhere. People who read only names and people who read descriptions are answering different questions.

What stays out on purpose

Wealth, power over others, status, fame and image are not added. In ACT they are goals or outcomes, not qualities of action, and in the research they relate to lower wellbeing through unmet needs. They are also the values most shaped by others’ approval. The proposed Influence and Providing cards give their healthier action forms a home.

How this was made

Two research briefs were written for this project, with citations checked against PubMed and publisher records. The card codings on this map are judgment calls based on those frameworks, not published mappings. A future option is to rebuild the map from real data, by looking at which cards people tend to place together, the same way Schwartz derived his circle.

The full briefs, with verification notes, are in the Lodestar repository under docs/research/.

Card names and wording on this page are kept in step with the app automatically: a check compares them with the deck whenever either changes. The codings, notes and the original deck’s counts are a record of the review and stay as they were. A third brief, on life areas, informed that feature.
