# Life areas belong beside the deck, not inside it

The proposal is valid in its core logic and needs changes in its details. ACT defines values as chosen qualities of ongoing action and treats life areas as the contexts where those qualities are lived. Every major ACT values tool (VLQ, Bull's-Eye, Personal Values Questionnaire) uses domains as scaffolding, not as values. So removing noun-domain cards such as Family from a quality-of-action deck, and adding life areas as a separate layer, fixes a real category error rather than inventing a new model. The psychometric evidence supports the structure but not the VLQ's scoring. Importance and "living it" behave as two separate scales. The 10-point format is finer than people can use reliably, and gap or product scores are unreliable. Ratings should therefore be shown as reflective prompts, never as a score. The cultural evidence supports making family an area and expressing devotion to it as a quality, but "Devotion to family" carries a duty reading that needs careful wording. Grief, infertility and illness work shows that an area which "doesn't apply" can be exactly where a person's values are. The engagement evidence is the main caution. A grid of repeated ratings placed after 15 to 20 minutes of sorting is where fatigue, straight-lining and mobile breakoff concentrate. No trial shows that domain-anchored values work improves committed action over domain-free work. The step is worth building as a short, optional, skippable bridge into action planning, not as an assessment.

## ACT theory treats areas as the stage, and qualities as the play

The theoretical case is the strongest part of the proposal. Hayes and colleagues define values as "chosen qualities of purposive action that can never be obtained as an object but can be instantiated moment by moment," and describe ACT exercises that help a client "choose life directions in various domains" such as family, career and spirituality ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)). The VLQ was built "directly from the primary text on ACT" and rates **importance and consistency across 10 domains** ([Wilson et al. 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF03395706)). The Personal Values Questionnaire describes each domain in a way meant to lead people "to write relatively ACT-consistent values," which uses the domain as a prompt for a quality ([ACBS PVQ page](https://contextualscience.org/personal_values_questionnaire_pvq)). The Bull's-Eye asks for a value written within each of four domains ([Lundgren et al. 2012](https://contextualscience.org/bulls_eye_values_survey_bevs)). Harris's values square uses work, love, health and play ([The Happiness Trap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Happiness_Trap)). Across all of these, the domain is the frame and the value is what goes inside it.

Lodestar's current deck mixes the two layers. The project's own language review flags Family, Friendship, Community, Health, Spirituality and Nature as domain or "goods" cards. Putting "Family vs Honesty" side by side is a category mismatch, and the card name signals the noun even when the description is written as an action (`docs/research/language-and-axiology.md`). The confusion is common even in the measurement literature: the 2026 Chinese VLQ paper describes core values "such as family, work, or spirituality" ([Song et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1760394)). Removing domain cards and moving them into an area list therefore aligns the app with ACT's own structure.

Two features of the proposal fit the theory better than the classic tools do. First, a quality can show up in several areas (patience in parenting and at work), and an area can hold several qualities. A many-to-many "attach 1 to 3 values per area" model reflects this better than the one-value-per-domain format that the Bull's-Eye and PVQ force. Second, Lodestar reverses the clinical order. It identifies qualities first and only then places them in areas. Nothing in the theory forbids this, and starting from qualities may reduce the "values are domains" conflation that a domain-first form invites. The one caveat is that the primary ACT texts (Wilson and Murrell, Dahl and colleagues' Life Compass, Wilson and DuFrene) could not be read for this review, so the theory rests on Hayes et al. and the instrument designs rather than on verbatim book guidance.

## The VLQ structure survives, but its scoring does not

Domain-based measures have weaker and more disputed psychometrics than domain-free ones. A review of 65 articles and 8 scales found that **the Valuing Questionnaire, Engaged Living Scale and Valued Living Scale "appear to have the strongest methodological support,"** leaving the VLQ and Bull's-Eye out of that group ([Reilly et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212144718302813)). A second review placed the VLQ and Bull's-Eye among the better tools while warning about the field's overall cohesiveness ([Barrett et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40732-019-00352-7)). Experts interviewed by Barney and colleagues said that "no measurement tool to date provides a complete and adequate assessment" of valuing, and called for more *contextual* measurement ([Barney et al. 2019](https://contextualscience.org/article/assessing_the_valuing_process_in_acceptance_and_commitment_therapy_experts)). That call is an argument for some area information, though not necessarily in VLQ form. The domain-based Valued Living Scale ranked well, which shows that domains alone do not cause weak psychometrics ([Jensen et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/pme.12802)). The VLQ's problems sit in its composite score and its instructions.

The specific problems map directly onto design choices. In a Black American sample, a one-factor model of VLQ composite scores **did not converge**, while importance and consistency each fit well as separate scales ([VanBuskirk et al. 2012](https://augusta.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/confirmatory-factor-analysis-of-the-valued-living-questionnaire-i)). In acquired brain injury, test-retest reliability was **.90 for importance but .58 for consistency**, and cognitive interviews found 11 comprehension errors involving the two-part structure, rating abstract values, and recalling the past week ([Miller et al. 2022](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/163319)). A comprehension-support version fixed these errors, reached composite ICC of .80, and performed comparably in neurotypical adults in about 2 minutes ([Wong et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/09638288.2024.2315510); [Skaliotis et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2025.09.006)). The Chinese validation found disordered thresholds on the 10-point scale and **favoured collapsing to 5 categories**. Its authors also note that the VLQ "was designed for responses to be interpreted qualitatively and not to be scored as an overall value," and that nothing stops a respondent answering 10 on every item ([Song et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1760394)).

Gap scores add a further problem. Difference scores compound the unreliability of their parts and hide which part drives an effect; the methodological fix is to analyse the components separately ([Edwards 2001](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/109442810143005)). Since consistency is the weaker component, any "importance minus living" number inherits its noise. Still, area ratings are not empty. A domain-based caregiving VLQ predicted depression, anxiety and life satisfaction "over and above the VQ" ([Morimoto et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/07317115.2022.2163951)). In an alcohol-specific VLQ, perceived value-behaviour discrepancy predicted readiness to change ([Miller et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000235)). The fair reading is that area ratings carry useful information when the areas match a person's real life, and that a visible gap works as a motivational prompt. Neither finding licenses a score, a cross-user comparison, or an outcome metric.

The weakest link is the claim that areas improve committed action. **No head-to-head trial compares domain-organised with domain-free values clarification.** The nearest evidence is that online values training plus goal setting raised next-semester GPA while goal setting alone did not, with no domain arm ([Chase et al. 2013](https://contextualscience.org/publications/values_are_not_just_goals_online_actbased_values_training_adds_goal_setting_improving)). The case for the step is therefore mechanistic: areas turn abstract qualities into concrete places to act. It is not yet evidential.

## Family works as an area, but "devotion" needs careful wording

The cultural evidence supports moving family out of the deck, because the culture-specific research breaks "family" into qualities that differ in how healthy they are. The Dual Filial Piety Model separates *reciprocal* filial piety, which is caring behaviour grounded in affection, from *authoritarian* filial piety, which historically required submission and suppression of autonomy ([Bedford and Yeh 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00100/text)). A meta-analysis of 39 studies found that familism had only **small** links to lower depression, suicide and internalizing problems, and none to substance use or externalizing problems ([Valdivieso-Mora et al. 2016](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.01632)). How someone lives out family matters, not only how strongly they hold it. In Schwartz's pan-cultural hierarchy, **benevolence, the care of close others, ranks first** across national samples ([Schwartz and Bardi 2001](https://repository.royalholloway.ac.uk/items/4c815cf2-e6e6-9a01-7090-90d03bbe5645/7/Schwartz_Bardi_2001_Value_Hierarchies_across_Cultures.pdf)). Treating care for family as a motivational quality fits that framework. Mapping that theory onto Lodestar is an inference, but it is a reasonable one.

This supports keeping a family-centred quality card so that users from collectivist backgrounds do not lose their most important value when the Family card goes. "Devotion to family" alone can read as duty or obligation. That suits authoritarian filial piety, but it may feel coercive to someone whose family harmed them. Wording that names the caring, reciprocal form and defines family broadly ("caring for and standing by the people I count as family") is a design inference, not a tested wording. Pairing it with cards for caring, gratitude or reciprocity, and respect for elders or tradition, lets users say *how* they value family. Cultural ACT adaptations consistently had to contextualise values, for example with older Chinese adults ([Leung et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1037/pst0000539)) and with Latinx families who built in familismo ([Hostetler 2022](https://scholarwolf.unr.edu/items/8496c6b6-69e4-4198-bab2-f0370bca8824)). The Aboriginal Stay Strong app starts by asking users to name "the people in their life that help keep them strong" before it explores family, cultural and spiritual areas ([Menzies School of Health Research](https://menzies.edu.au/page/Research/Projects/Mental_Health_and_wellbeing/Development_of_the_Stay_Strong_iPad_App)). Self-defined areas, and the option to rename or add areas, follow from this.

Applicability is the sharper sensitivity issue. ACT practice treats values tied to loss as valid and often central. Bereavement practitioners describe helping people "gradually identify, reconnect with, and act in line with their values after loss," adapted to their sensitivities ([Willi et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12904-024-01390-x)). An infertility ACT program includes values "that may be neglected due to infertility" ([Balsom et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12978-024-01766-5)). For people with chronic illness, valued-living areas covering health and productivity link to pain interference and depression ([Jensen et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0898264319861006)). So Work and Health may be exactly where loss sits. A single "doesn't apply" option merges "not relevant to me" with "matters, but painful or out of reach right now." A "how fully are you living this" rating can feel like a verdict to a bereaved parent asked about Parenting. No study tested alternative wordings, but SAMHSA's trauma-informed principles of safety, transparency, and voice and choice point to optional, explained, per-item skips that carry no penalty ([youth.gov summary of SAMHSA 2014](https://youth.gov/feature-article/samhsas-concept-trauma-and-guidance-trauma-informed-approach)). A scoping review of 118 design studies found that trauma-informed principles are rarely applied explicitly in technology ([DOAJ record](https://matdisc.unitech.ac.pg/Record/doaj-art-679d0d25d3874d70ac2bd330ba003d56/Description)), so this is a gap Lodestar would be filling by design rather than by copying.

## A late 5-minute grid is the riskiest place to add burden

Lodestar is a single sitting, so the relevant risk is breakoff within the session, not weeks of attrition. The good news is that focused single-session online work can be finished by most people: **90% completed** an ACT- and MI-informed single session with values content ([Tobias et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/01454455211040051)), and free single-session interventions produced measurable 3-month effects in 2,452 adolescents ([Schleider et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01235-0)). Acceptance-based trials also had lower attrition across 70 app RCTs ([Linardon and Fuller-Tyszkiewicz 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000459)). The bad news is that the proposed step has the burden profile that survey research flags. When the announced web-survey length rose, fewer people started and finished, and later questions drew faster, lower-quality answers ([Galesic and Bosnjak 2009](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/galesic-bosnjak-effectsofquestionnairelengthonparticipationandindicatorsofresponsequalityinawebsurvey-2009)). Satisficing, including straight-lined ratings, rises as motivation falls and task difficulty rises ([Krosnick 1991](https://scilit.com/publications/bb86ba8f6056bbb9e4d50a34f0e2c674)). Mobile respondents break off more often, and simpler, mobile-optimised design reduces this ([Mavletova and Couper 2015](https://dx.doi.org/10.5334/bar.f)). A two-dimension rating grid across about 10 areas, followed by value-to-area assignment, after 15 to 20 minutes of sorting, sits where all three effects meet.

Relevance can offset some of this. A meta-analysis found lower response rates for longer questionnaires but concluded that "it is impossible to separate the impact of content from length," and recommended deciding on content ([Rolstad et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jval.2011.06.003)). A step that visibly feeds the user's own action plan is likely to feel less like a questionnaire. In psychological e-therapies, module completion was the adherence measure most tied to outcomes ([Donkin et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1772)). This argues for protecting the core path (sort, choose, rank, act) and keeping the new step from standing between the user and the action step.

The action link is real but smaller than it looks. If-then plans that specify when, where and how improve goal attainment (d = .65 across 94 tests ([Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)); d = .27 to .66 across 642 tests ([Sheeran et al. 2024](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/69905))). But "Work" is not a situational cue. Most of the gain from "Connection, Family, call Mum on Sunday after lunch" comes from the concrete action and cue, which a when-and-where prompt in the existing action step would deliver with or without areas. The area label probably helps users think of concrete situations, but no trial has tested that. Personalization offers no guaranteed boost either. User choice is the most common personalization mechanism in depression apps, yet evidence for its benefits was "scarce and inconclusive" ([Hornstein et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3389/fdgth.2023.1170002)), even though users cite lack of personalization as a barrier and a sense of control as a facilitator ([Borghouts et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.2196/24387)). The step should be justified by clearer actions, not by expected engagement gains.

## Design implications

The evidence turns into a fairly specific set of decisions. The table states each decision and the evidence behind it. Rows marked as inference have no direct test.

| Decision | Recommendation | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Deck | Remove Family, and review the other domain-like cards (Friendship, Community, Health, Spirituality, Nature) the same way. Replace each with a quality wording where a quality exists, so that users who hold it as a core value keep a card | ACT definitions; internal language review |
| "Devotion to family" | Keep it, worded around caring and standing by "the people I count as family," and pair it with caring, gratitude or reciprocity, and tradition cards | Dual Filial Piety; familism meta-analysis (wording is inference) |
| Placement | After ranking, offered as an explicit choice ("Add life areas, about 5 minutes" or "Go to actions"). Never a gate before the action step | Breakoff and fatigue evidence; Donkin |
| Area list | Pick areas rather than rate all 10. Allow renaming and custom areas. Use neutral order and inclusive definitions (chosen and extended family, paid or unpaid work, health "as it is") | Domain fit drives incremental validity; Stay Strong; inference |
| Not applicable | Offer two neutral options, "Not part of my life right now" and "Matters to me, but it's hard right now." Score neither as low importance | Bereavement and infertility ACT practice; SAMHSA (wording untested) |
| Ratings | One area per screen. Plain 5-point labelled scales. Importance and "living it" shown as two separate marks. The second rating is optional, has an explicit time frame, and is framed gently | VLQ factor structure; Rasch; VLQ-CS; sensitivity evidence |
| Scores | No composite, product, gap score, or "valued-living score." Highlight high-importance, low-living areas only as a suggested place to act | Edwards; VLQ authors' qualitative intent; VLQ alcohol readiness finding |
| Values per area | Many-to-many, 1 to 3 core values per area, drawn from the user's own ranked list | ACT cross-domain qualities |
| Actions | Action = value + optional area + concrete "when and where." Add the when-and-where prompt to the action step for everyone, not only area users | Implementation-intention meta-analyses |
| Global check | If an overall "how am I living my values" item is wanted, use a domain-free Progress and Obstruction framing, after checking licensing | VQ psychometrics and cross-cultural invariance |
| Skips | Skipping the step or any area costs nothing: no warnings, badges or "incomplete" profile | SAMHSA voice and choice (untested in apps) |
| Measurement | Track opt-in rate, step drop-off, time per step and straight-lining by device. Test wording through cognitive interviews with bereaved, childless, estranged and collectivist-background users | Evidence gaps listed above |

Two tensions remain open. Removing Health, Spirituality or Community cards is cleaner in theory but risks erasing values that some users experience as qualities rather than places, especially faith. The safer route is renaming over deleting. And Lodestar's reverse order (qualities first, areas second) is theoretically sound but untested. If analytics show many users attach the same one or two values to every area, the step is adding labels without adding thought, and it should shrink to a single "where will you live this value?" field in the action builder.

## Conclusion

The question is less "are life areas valid?" and more "what job should they do?" ACT, the psychometric record and the sensitivity evidence all point to the same answer. Areas are valid as a context for placing qualities and choosing where to act. They are not valid as a scored assessment of how well someone is living, and they are least safe as a fixed checklist that assumes a partner, children, a job and good health. This changes the build from a VLQ clone into a lightweight bridge between ranking and acting, with the measurement stripped out and the user's own definitions put in.

Because no trial shows that domain anchoring improves committed action, Lodestar's own usage data will be the first real evidence on that question. A cheap test is available. Add the when-and-where prompt to the action step for everyone, offer the life-areas step as an option, and compare action specificity and completion between users who take the step and users who skip it. If the area step adds little beyond the cue prompt, the theory is satisfied by the deck fix alone, and the 5 minutes can go back to the user.
