# Research brief: language and philosophy of value in the Lodestar deck

*Compiled November 2026 as a companion to `docs/RESEARCH.md`. That brief covers ACT's definition of values, pliance and the card-sort literature, and none of it is repeated here. This one asks what linguistics (semantics, pragmatics) and philosophy of value (axiology) say about the 65 cards in `assets/js/values.js` and the current flow: three piles (Very / Somewhat / Not important to me), then a core set of up to 10, then a ranking.*

*How citations were checked: PubMed for indexed items (PMIDs given), and web search against publisher, PhilPapers/PhilArchive, library-catalogue and journal records for everything else. This session could not reach plato.stanford.edu, api.crossref.org or doi.org, so some DOIs could not be resolved. A DOI tagged **[DOI unconfirmed]** is the publisher's standard DOI as I know it but was not seen in a search result. **[unverified]** marks a claim I could not confirm at all. A summary of a book's argument that I could not check against the text is marked **[from secondary sources]**.*

## 1. Axiology: what kind of thing is on each card?

**Intrinsic, final and instrumental value.** Moore (1903, ch. VI) asked what is good "in itself" and answered with an *isolation test*: imagine the thing existing on its own and ask whether it would still be good. His leading candidates were personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment **[section numbers from secondary sources]**. Korsgaard (1983) argued that two distinctions get run together. One is *final versus instrumental* (valued as an end versus as a means). The other is *intrinsic versus extrinsic* (good because of its own properties versus because of how it relates to something else, such as a person who chooses it). A thing can be valued as an end and still owe its goodness to its relation to an agent. This fits ACT closely. Wilson's definition says values are "chosen" and that their reinforcement is "intrinsic in engagement". In Korsgaard's terms that is a claim about *final* value which is *extrinsically* grounded: grounded in choice, not in some objective property. The deck mixes items people usually hold as ends (Beauty, Love, Curiosity) with items people often hold as means (Health, Safety, Order, Self-discipline, Learning, Leadership). Two people can put Health in "Very important" for different reasons, one as an end and one as a means to being around for their children. The sort cannot tell them apart.

**Monism versus pluralism, and incomparability.** Berlin (1958/1969) held that ultimate values are many, can conflict, and cannot all be had together. Raz (1986, ch. 13) distinguished incommensurability (no common measure) and added *constitutive* incommensurability: for some goods, refusing to trade them against others is part of what valuing them means. Raz's example is that friendship is not for sale. Chang (1997) collected the debate. Chang (2002) argued for a fourth comparative relation, *on a par*: two options neither better, worse nor equal, yet still comparable. A strict drag-rank of the top values assumes a complete ordering. Pluralists would say that ordering "Honesty" against "Family" may be underdetermined, or even something the person resists on principle.

**Objective-list theories.** Parfit (1984, Appendix I, pp. 493–502) sorted theories of what makes a life go well into hedonistic, desire-fulfilment and objective-list theories. Finnis (1980) lists basic goods: life, knowledge, play, aesthetic experience, sociability/friendship, practical reasonableness and religion (later work adds marriage). Frankena's (1973, pp. 87–88) long list includes health, pleasure, knowledge, beauty, virtue, love and friendship, freedom, peace and security, adventure and novelty, and good reputation, honour and esteem. Many Lodestar cards appear on these lists. That reveals a tension. ACT treats values as freely chosen, a subjectivist stance close to desire or endorsement theories. But a *curated deck* is in effect an objective list. It decides in advance which goods count, and it presents only flattering ones. Frankena's "power", "achievement" and "good reputation, honor, esteem" have no counterparts, and neither do Schwartz's (1992) Power, Achievement and Conformity value types. That is a defensible ACT choice (outcomes are not values), but it should be a stated choice. It also feeds the ceiling problem in §3.

**Thick and thin concepts.** Williams (1985) contrasted *thin* evaluative terms ("good", "right") with *thick* ones ("courage", "brutality", "gratitude") that are both "world-guided" and "action-guiding". Most card names are thick: Courage, Honesty, Loyalty, Generosity. Several descriptions, though, fall back on *thin* terms that assume the very thing being sorted. Justice has "what is right", Leadership "something worthwhile", Simplicity "what truly matters", Courage and Persistence "what matters", Integrity "my principles". These phrases are circular in a values deck. Väyrynen (2013) argues that the evaluation in thick terms is largely *pragmatic*, coming from conversational norms rather than from word meaning. If so, "Loyalty" or "Tradition" can be condemnatory in some contexts (loyalty to an abusive group) without any change in meaning. Kirchin (2013, 2017) defends *non-separationism*: you cannot cleanly strip the evaluative part from the descriptive part. One consequence is that rewording a card never yields a neutral description. The aim should be descriptions that tell the reader *which actions* count, not descriptions free of evaluation.

**Virtues, goods, practices and relations.** MacIntyre (1981; p. 187 in the 3rd ed.) defines a practice as a cooperative activity with internal goods and standards of excellence. A virtue is an acquired quality that enables a person to achieve those goods (p. 191). By that scheme the deck contains at least five kinds of thing (my classification):

- **Virtues or dispositions:** Honesty, Courage, Patience, Humility, Generosity, Fairness, Loyalty, Reliability, Kindness and others, about half the deck.
- **Goods or objects of value:** Beauty, Nature, Pleasure, Health, Safety, Freedom, Justice, Tradition.
- **States:** Peace, and partly Growth and Simplicity.
- **Relations or life domains:** Family, Friendship, Community, Intimacy, Connection.
- **Activities or practices:** Learning, Mastery, Adventure, Leadership, Mindfulness, Self-care.

ACT's own measures treat Family, Friendship, Community, Health and Spirituality as *domains* (the VLQ's life areas), not qualities. Putting a domain and a quality on equal footing ("Family vs Honesty") is a category mismatch. The descriptions mostly fix this by turning goods into actions ("Investing time, care and energy in my family relationships"), which is the right repair. The name on the card still signals the noun, though, and most users read the name first.

**Caring and second-order endorsement.** Frankfurt (1971) separates first-order desires from second-order volitions, wanting to have a desire. Frankfurt (1982) argues that what we *care about* shapes a self, and that caring is a matter of identification, not mere liking. ACT's "chosen" values are closer to Frankfurt's identification than to preference. A pile labelled "Very important" invites a first-order rating. A Frankfurt-style follow-up for each core value would be "Is this something I *want to be moved by*, even when I'm not?" That supports the pliance checks already planned, without duplicating them.

**What a philosopher would say the deck conflates or leaves out:**

1. Final and instrumental value.
2. Virtues, goods, states, relations and activities.
3. Being good *to* others and being treated well (see §2 on direction).
4. Value *for me* (prudential) and value *full stop* (moral). Pleasure and Fun are prudential. Justice and Honesty claim moral authority, so endorsing them carries a social cost or reward the others lack.
5. It leaves out the less flattering goods (achievement, recognition, influence, comfort) and values that conflict with each other.

## 2. Semantics: what the words mean, and to whom

**Polysemy, vagueness, gradability.** Most value names are nominalizations of gradable adjectives (*kind → kindness*, *honest → honesty*). Kennedy (2007) shows that relative gradable adjectives have a context-dependent threshold: how kind counts as "kind"? Absolute ones are tied to a scale endpoint. Nominalizing hides both the threshold and the *scope*: kind to whom, honest about what. Some descriptions push items toward an absolute, maximal reading ("even when no one is watching", "even when I feel afraid"). That invites perfectionistic, rule-like readings, the "should" ACT warns against. Many names are also polysemous:

- **Love:** romantic, familial, or universal (*agape*).
- **Respect:** Darwall (1977) separates *recognition respect*, owed to every person, from *appraisal respect*, earned by merit.
- **Responsibility:** being accountable for one's choices, versus duties of care for others.
- **Acceptance:** approval, resignation, ACT willingness, or *being accepted*. The original PVCS card reads "to be accepted as I am" (see RESEARCH.md).
- **Order:** tidiness, versus social or political order.
- **Peace:** inner calm, interpersonal harmony, or political peace.
- **Nature:** the natural world, versus human nature.
- **Discipline:** self-control, versus punishment.

**Directional ambiguity.** Many value words name a relation with two sides. *Respect*, *trust*, *acceptance*, *love*, *loyalty*, *forgiveness* and *fairness* can each be something I give or something I get. Baier (1986) treats trust as letting others take care of things we value, which is a vulnerability you *extend*. Being trustworthy is a different disposition. The Trust card explicitly asks for both ("Being trustworthy, and extending trust to others"). Read without its description, "Respect" may be heard as "being respected". That is an outcome you cannot control and, in ACT terms, not a value. The descriptions settle the direction for most cards, but only if people read them.

**Prototypes.** Rosch (1975) showed that categories have graded internal structure around prototypical members. Prototype analyses of everyday relational concepts, including love (Fehr, 1988), forgiveness (Kearns & Fincham, 2004) and respect (Frei & Shaver, 2002), find lay concepts organized around central features, not definitions. Fehr reports that *trust* and *caring* were among the most central features of love **[from secondary sources]**. Two lessons follow. A one-line description picks a few features and so can shift a word away from its prototype for a given reader: "Kindness … gentle" leaves out firm or tough kindness. And neighbouring cards share central features: Caring, Compassion, Kindness, Empathy, Nurturing and Love all draw on the same prototype cluster.

**Cross-cultural non-equivalence (NSM).** Wierzbicka's Natural Semantic Metalanguage work argues that many value words treated as universal are culturally specific. Freedom differs from Latin *libertas*, Russian *svoboda* and Polish *wolność* (Wierzbicka, 1997, ch. 3). *Fair* and *fairness* are described as "key Anglo values" with no close equivalents in most languages (Wierzbicka, 2006, ch. 5). Emotion terms such as *Angst* do not map one-to-one across languages (Wierzbicka, 1999). This matters for any card that names an emotion or emotion-laden state, such as Peace, Gratitude, Compassion and Pleasure. Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan (2010) document how unrepresentative WEIRD samples are on fairness, cooperation and self-concept. Cards that encode a particular Anglo or individualist self-ideal include Fairness, Freedom, Independence, Assertiveness, Authenticity and Self-care. A Western user will hear them as neutral. Many others will not, and translation will not fix that.

**Conceptual metaphor.** Lakoff (1996) argues that moral reasoning runs on systematic metaphors. *Moral accounting* treats well-being as wealth and morality as balancing books. Other metaphors cluster under "Strict Father" (moral strength, self-discipline) and "Nurturant Parent" (care, empathy) models **[chapter-level detail not re-verified]**. The deck's wording draws on both: "giving back" (Community), "letting go of resentment" (Forgiveness, which cancels a debt), "Investing time" (Family), "standing on my own two feet" (Independence), "even-handedly" (Fairness), "Keeping going … despite setbacks" (Persistence). The metaphors aren't wrong. They do carry framings that resonate with some political and cultural outlooks more than others.

## 3. Pragmatics: what endorsing a card *does*

**Value words as expressives.** Emotivism (Stevenson, 1937) and prescriptivism (Hare, 1952) hold that evaluative words have commendatory "emotive" or prescriptive meaning beyond their descriptive content. Potts (2007) gives a linguistic account of expressive meaning as a separate dimension. On any of these views, every Lodestar card is a "hooray" word. Putting it in "Not important to me" is pragmatically marked, close to saying you don't care about honesty. "Not important to me" puts disvaluing and mere low priority on the same pile. The Schwartz Value Survey, by contrast, offers a −1 "opposed to my values" option alongside 0 "not important".

**Social desirability and self-presentation.** Paulhus (1984) separates impression management from self-deceptive enhancement. Schwartz, Verkasalo, Antonovsky and Sagiv (1997) found that social desirability relates to value ratings mostly *substantively*: high scorers really do prioritize conformity and benevolence-type values. They found only weak evidence of a general inflation bias ("much substance, some style"). For a self-guided app the lesson is to expect some inflation on moral-sounding cards (Honesty, Integrity, Kindness) and not to treat a full "Very" pile as an error. The deck has no low-desirability cards, so it gives a person few honest ways to differentiate.

**Implicature in descriptions.** Under Grice's (1975) maxims, readers assume each added clause is relevant, so a description doesn't only define the word: it *says something about it*. Freedom's "and respecting other people's right to do the same" suggests that freedom without that restraint is not endorsed. It moralizes the card and makes it double-barreled. Authenticity's "not who I think I should be" suggests a stance on social expectations. Integrity's "even when no one is watching" suggests the norm is under threat. Some of this is deliberate anti-pliance wording and may be worth keeping. The point is that what a user endorses is the *description* as much as the word. Users who scan names and users who read descriptions are effectively answering different items.

**Framing and context.** Self-reports shift with wording, response format and context (Schwarz, 1999; Tourangeau, Rips & Rasinski, 2000). Equivalent descriptions can produce different choices (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981). In a card sort the earlier cards set the context for later ones. Shuffling order per session, which the app's `state.order` makes possible, spreads order effects across sessions instead of building in one bias.

**Double-barreled items.** Questionnaire guidance warns against items that ask about two things at once (Bradburn, Sudman & Wansink, 2004 **[location of this guidance in the book not verified]**). Menold (2020) found experimentally that double-barreled items differed from single-element versions in means and latent parameters and failed measurement-invariance tests. At least one single-element version was more valid. When a card bundles two targets (self and others) or two acts (noticing and expressing), people tend to answer whichever element is more salient. About 20 cards bundle in this way (see the table).

**Ceiling effects, ranking versus rating.** Value ratings pile up at the top end. Alwin and Krosnick (1985) found that ratings and rankings agreed on aggregate order but differed in latent structure. Krosnick and Alwin (1988) found that removing *non-differentiating* raters made rating results look like ranking results. McCarty and Shrum (2000) showed that asking people first to choose their *most* and *least* important values, then rate, reduced end-piling and increased differentiation. The Schwartz Value Survey uses a similar anchoring step: pick the most important value, then the one most opposed, then rate the rest, keeping 7 for "ordinarily no more than two" values. Schwartz recommends centering scores on each person's mean rating (MRAT) to remove scale-use differences (GESIS ZIS documentation; Schwartz's ESS scoring note **[original PDF not retrieved]**). Best-worst scaling reproduced the Schwartz structure and took less respondent time (Lee, Soutar & Louviere, 2008). The trade-off: fully ipsative scores sum to a constant. That limits comparisons between people and builds in negative correlations (Hicks, 1970). This is fine for self-reflection and a problem only if Lodestar ever reports norms.

**Noun, verb and "being" framing.** Bryan, Walton, Rogers and Dweck (2011) found that "being a voter" (noun) raised registration interest and turnout compared with "voting" (verb). Bryan, Adams and Monin (2013) found that "don't be a cheater" reduced cheating compared with "don't cheat". Bryan, Master and Walton (2014) found that "being a helper" increased young children's helping. But a large field replication found no turnout difference between noun and verb wording (Gerber, Huber, Biggers & Hendry, 2016). And asking children to "be helpers" *backfired* after setbacks (Foster-Hanson, Cimpian, Leshin & Rhodes, 2020). Walton and Banaji (2004) report that noun labels strengthen how people construe their preferences **[findings not verified; no abstract was retrievable]**. For an ACT deck the evidence points one way. Identity framing ("Being someone people can count on", "Being genuine") may motivate in the moment but is fragile after lapses, and lapses are certain in values work. ACT's own guidance favours verbs and adverbs, which are also robust to failure. Six descriptions start with "Being" + adjective: Authenticity, Connection, Honesty, Kindness, Reliability and Trust. Reliability ("Being someone people can count on") is also framed from other people's point of view.

## 4. Design implications

**Wording rules for card descriptions**

1. **One act, one target.** Each description names a single kind of action toward a single target. Where self and others matter, split the card (Forgiveness / Self-forgiveness) or pick one.
2. **Verbs over "Being".** Start with an action verb or gerund ("Telling the truth…", "Keeping my word…"), not "Being + adjective".
3. **State the direction.** For relational words, make the user the agent of the giving (Respect: "Treating people as…"). Never write a value that depends on how others treat the user.
4. **No thin, circular terms.** Replace "what matters", "what is right" and "something worthwhile" with concrete scope.
5. **No maximal qualifiers by default.** "Even when no one is watching" is a stringent standard. Use it only where it defines the concept (Integrity), and consider moving such clauses into reflection prompts.
6. **No feeling states as the value.** "Calm" (Peace, Patience) is a feeling. Describe the action instead ("Responding without lashing out…").
7. **Plain, translatable words.** NSM-style wording with simple, near-universal vocabulary (do, say, think, want, good, people, other) makes translation and cross-cultural reading easier.
8. **Show name and description together everywhere.** Including the ranking and summary screens, so the item being answered stays the same.

**Reducing ceiling effects**

- Add a most/least anchoring step before or within the 3-pile sort: "Pick the 3 cards that matter most and the 3 that matter least to you right now" (McCarty & Shrum, 2000).
- Keep MAX_CORE = 10. Consider a soft quota for "Very" (e.g., a nudge when more than about a third of the deck is in "Very"), framed as prioritizing, not rejecting.
- Offer best-worst trials over 4–5 cards as an optional tie-breaker for an oversized "Very" pile (Lee et al., 2008).
- Relabel "Not important to me" as "Less important to me right now", or split it into "Less important" and "Not for me". That separates low priority from disvaluing and lowers the expressive cost.
- In ranking, allow ties or "on a par" groups at the top (Chang, 2002). An enforced strict order adds noise and can feel morally wrong to users who see their top values as incommensurable (Raz, 1986).
- If you ever show numbers, center them on each person's mean and present them as within-person only (Schwartz; Hicks, 1970).
- Consider 3–5 legitimate but less flattering action-quality cards (e.g., *Achievement*, *Recognition*, *Influence*, *Comfort*, *Competition*) so the deck covers more of the Schwartz circle and endorsement means more.

**Cultural neutrality**

- Mark the culturally specific cards (Fairness, Freedom, Independence, Assertiveness, Authenticity, Spirituality, Tradition, Family). Offer collectivist or relational counterparts (e.g., *Harmony*, *Duty*, *Belonging*, *Interdependence*).
- Define Family inclusively ("the people I consider family") and give it a trauma-aware note.
- Keep custom cards prominent. They are the best safeguard against a fixed list built on one culture's assumptions.

**Qualities of action or goods?** Keep ACT's quality-of-action descriptions. They resolve most of the category mixing in §1 and match the technical definition. But be open about it: either rename domain and good cards with action nouns (e.g., Family → *Family care*; avoid "Being there for…" given the identity-framing issue), or label domains as domains and let the user attach qualities to them, as the VLQ does. Add an optional "for its own sake or for something else?" prompt (Korsgaard/Moore) for each core value. When the answer is "something else", ask what that something else is. It may be the real value.

### Card-level observations

Issue types: **DB** = double-barreled (two acts or two targets); **DIR** = directional ambiguity; **POLY** = polysemy or vagueness; **CAT** = category (domain, good, state, role or process rather than quality of action); **CULT** = culturally loaded; **THIN** = circular or thin evaluative term; **ID** = "Being"/identity framing; **IMP** = implicature or added stance; **FEEL** = feeling state; **OVL** = heavy overlap with another card.

| Card | Issue type | Suggestion |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptance | DIR, POLY, DB | PVCS sense is "being accepted"; deck sense is ACT willingness, aimed at three targets (myself, others, life). Pick one target, or split into *Acceptance* (of experience) and *Accepting others*. Avoid any reading as approval or resignation. |
| Assertiveness | CULT, DB | An Anglo/individualist norm; may read as rude elsewhere. "Needs and boundaries" covers two acts. Consider "Saying clearly what I need, while staying respectful." |
| Authenticity | CULT, IMP, ID | "Not who I think I should be" adds an anti-conformity stance; "Being genuine" is an identity frame. Try "Acting in ways that fit who I really am." |
| Beauty | DB, OVL | "Noticing, savoring and creating" mixes appreciation with making (overlaps Creativity). Keep appreciation; leave making to Creativity. |
| Challenge | CAT, OVL | "Help me grow" adds an outcome or means (overlaps Growth). End at "…that stretch me." |
| Community | DB, CAT | Domain plus two acts. Choose "Taking part in…" or "Giving back to…". "Giving back" is moral-accounting language. |
| Connection | ID, CAT, OVL | The name suggests a relationship you receive; the description (present-moment attention) overlaps Mindfulness. Try "Giving people my full attention when we're together." |

| Independence | DB, CULT | Thinking for myself versus practical self-reliance; individualist ideal. Pick one; offer *Interdependence* as a counterpart. |
| Integrity | THIN, IMP | "My principles" is thin; "even when no one is watching" sets a maximal standard. Acceptable since it is close to the definition; avoid that phrasing elsewhere. |
| Intimacy | DB, POLY | Emotional or physical; "someone" assumes a partner. Consider separate emotional and physical cards, or neutral wording. |
| Justice | THIN, DB, CULT, OVL | "What is right" is thin; two acts; politically loaded; overlaps Fairness. Try "Standing up for people who are treated unfairly." |

| Leadership | CAT, THIN | A role that assumes followers; "something worthwhile" is thin. Try "Helping a group find and move toward shared aims." |
| Love | POLY, DB, THIN | Circular ("acting with love"); romantic, familial or universal; others and self. Use non-circular wording: "Showing affection and care for the people I love." |
| Loyalty | DB, POLY | People versus causes; can be condemnatory (loyalty to harmful groups). Add "…when they deserve it", or keep the scope to people. |

| Order | POLY | Personal tidiness versus social order; the description settles it. Keep. |
| Patience | FEEL | "Calmly" names a feeling. Try "Giving things and people the time they need." |
| Peace | CAT, FEEL, DB, POLY | A state; inner calm plus interpersonal harmony; political sense. Try *Harmony* ("Acting to reduce conflict around me"). |
| Persistence | THIN, OVL | "What matters" is thin; overlaps Self-discipline and Diligence. Consider merging into a "follow-through" cluster. |
| Reliability | ID, DIR | Reputational, third-person framing ("someone people can count on"). Try "Doing what I said I would do." |
| Respect | DIR, POLY | Given versus received; recognition versus appraisal respect (Darwall). The description fixes the direction. Keep the agent wording; consider separate *Self-respect*. |
| Responsibility | POLY | Accountability versus duty of care. The description settles it. Keep. |
| Safety | CAT, DB | A state or good; self plus others. For anxious users, safety-seeking can serve experiential avoidance. Try "Protecting the people in my care." |
| Simplicity | DB, THIN | Material minimalism versus focus; "what truly matters" is circular. Try "Living with less clutter and fewer distractions." |
| Spirituality | POLY, CULT | Vague; merges or replaces religion (Finnis's "religion" good). Offer *Faith/Religion* separately. |
| Tradition | CULT, POLY | Can be heard as conservative or ethnic. Fine as written; consider pairing with *Heritage*. |
| Trust | DB, DIR, ID | Two opposite directions in one card (Baier). Split into *Trustworthiness* ("Keeping my word and others' confidences") and *Trusting others*. |
| Wisdom | DB, THIN | Seeking understanding plus judging well; abstract. Keep "Acting with good judgment"; leave seeking to Learning. |
| Caring / Compassion / Kindness / Empathy / Love / Nurturing | OVL | Six cards from one prototype cluster split users' choices among them. Consider merging two or three, or grouping them visually. |
| Self-care / Self-compassion / Health | OVL | Unclear boundaries. Define Health as body, Self-care as routines and rest, and Self-compassion as stance. |
| Fun / Humor / Pleasure | OVL | Hedonic cluster. Fine; make sure the descriptions differ clearly. |

Mastery was later flagged in feedback; see the addendum below. Hospitality norms vary across cultures, but the description is neutral enough.

### Addendum (November 2026): Mastery

Feedback flagged *Mastery* as a word some people hear through "master" and its history in slavery. This is a culturally loaded (CULT) problem the review above missed: the card's description was sound, but the name carries associations for some readers that its intended sense does not. Etymology does not settle it, because the skill sense ("mastery of a craft") and the dominion sense ("master of others") share a root, and readers meet the name before the description. Other fields have been retiring "master" in technical terms, while psychology still uses "mastery" widely (mastery goals; Pearlin's Mastery Scale; the "mastery criteria" of behavioral treatments). Reports that Parent-Child Interaction Therapy has dropped the term could not be confirmed from the PCIT training materials we found, which still use "mastery criteria".

The name was also polysemous (POLY) in a way that matters for sorting. In psychology, "mastery" covers both skill built through practice (a *mastery goal*; Ames, 1992) and a sense of control over one's life (Pearlin & Schooler, 1978; Ryff's "environmental mastery"). Deck version 3 splits the card: **Craft** ("Refining my skills through steady practice.") and **Resourcefulness** ("Looking for practical ways to deal with problems as they come."). See `docs/VALUES-MAP.md` for the full rationale.
